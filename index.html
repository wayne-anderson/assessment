What this is

Good course design is our strongest move

The Office of the Provost and EdPlus have launched a collaborative effort, including workshops, listening sessions, and resource development, aimed at supporting faculty in developing effective ways to cultivate and assess our students’ learning during this transformational moment. The effort will grow and take shape based on faculty input.

These strategies and frameworks shaped what we ran in July: the Assessment Design Accelerator, a two-day event with faculty invited from the top 100 enrolling ASU Online courses. About 25 participants joined us this summer, and it is something we are exploring doing more of going forward. Along the way we have done the researching, reading, and testing so faculty do not have to start from scratch.

A through-line to hold in mind: cheating prevention is a function of design, not solely of enforcement. The strategies below redesign assignments and assessments so that authentic student learning becomes easier to see, including at scale, and so students are empowered to create work worth doing regardless of what AI can produce.

Our conviction runs through all of it: good course design is our strongest move in this environment. When we invest in it, we are in a much better position to meet the challenges AI has surfaced.

Two anchor frameworks

Two frameworks that anchor the work

They answer different questions. Held together, they turn abstract redesign into a single, concrete design sentence for every assignment.

TILT

Transparency in Learning and Teaching

A small, high-leverage change to how assignments are written. Many students, especially those first in their family to attend college, struggle not because the work is too hard but because the unwritten rules are unclear. TILT makes three things explicit on every assignment:

  • Purpose. Why am I doing this? The skills practiced, knowledge gained, and connection to course outcomes.
  • Task. What exactly do I do? The steps, what to avoid, and any productive-struggle left open on purpose.
  • Criteria. How is it judged? A checklist or rubric with annotated examples of successful work.

An AAC&U pilot across 7 minority-serving institutions found that TILTing just two assignments produced significant gains in confidence, belonging, and skill mastery, with the largest gains for first-generation and underserved students. It is transparency, not more work.

Two-Lane

Assessment must do two jobs at once

From the University of Sydney (Liu & Bridgeman). Assessment now has to both assure that students achieved the outcomes and help them learn to work with AI. One assessment type cannot do both, so every assessment is sorted into one lane:

  • Lane 1: Controlled. Conditions controlled enough to give a defensible signal of the student’s own capability. This is about conditions, not location, so it fits async online. Controlled does not mean AI-banned.
  • Lane 2: Open. AI use is permitted and encouraged. The focus shifts from policing AI to building AI literacy, with authentic, process-focused tasks. This is the home of most coursework.

Assign lanes assessment by assessment. Put controlled conditions only where an outcome must be assured, and let most of the course live in the open lane. A single task can combine both: an open project capped by a short controlled check.

The design
summary

Two-Lane decides which lane a task lives in. TILT makes the rules of that lane explicit, especially in the open lane. Together: “This is a Lane [1 or 2] assignment, scored on Purpose / Task / Criteria.” When a faculty member can produce that summary, redesign becomes concrete.
