Nine practical strategies for assessment design in the age of AI

Each strategy comes from real faculty who have published protocols, materials, or working models you can adapt for your course. You do not need all nine. One move from a few different clusters often produces the largest change with the least redesign effort.

Two foundational frameworks

The frameworks that anchor these strategies

The strategies fit into two composable frameworks that come from established faculty practice. Neither is required for adopting any single strategy, but holding both in mind sharpens decisions about where to invest redesign effort.

TILT

Transparency in Learning and Teaching

Developed by Mary-Ann Winkelmes beginning in 2009. Its premise: most assignment failures are failures of student-facing clarity, not student capability. TILT asks faculty to make three things explicit on every assignment:

  • Purpose. Why this assignment? What course outcomes does it serve, and what skill or knowledge does it build that students will use beyond the course?
  • Task. What specifically to do, in what steps, in what format. Process is named, not assumed.
  • Criteria. What success looks like. A rubric or checklist with examples of more and less successful work.

More than 15 years of IRB-approved, multi-institutional research documents that TILT-redesigned assignments produce measurable gains in academic confidence, sense of belonging, and skill mastery, with particular benefit for first-generation and underrepresented students. It closes equity gaps without lowering rigor. TILT clarity is the precondition for everything else: Torrey Trust’s question, “AI can do this. Why am I asking them to do this?” is the TILT Purpose question in another form.

Where to look

  • TILT Higher Ed Resources — templates, examples, and the Winkelmes team’s ongoing work.
  • Measuring Transparency: A Learning-Focused Assignment Rubric (Palmer, Gravett, LaFleur 2018) — a 19-component instrument to audit your own assignments.
  • Starting move: pick one assignment you have this term. Score it against Purpose / Task / Criteria. Rewrite whichever component is weakest.
Two-Lane

Programmatic assessment separation

Developed by Danny Liu at the University of Sydney and extended by Cath Ellis at Western Sydney University. The model separates all assessment activity in a course or program into two lanes:

  • Lane 1: Secured assurance of learning. The one or two moments in a course (or two to four in a program) where the institution needs to be confident the student actually did the work. Oral defenses, supervised tasks, in-person presentations, proctored capstones. Designed for security and observability.
  • Lane 2: Formative, AI-rich, repeatable practice. Everything else. Students experiment, revise, and fail cheaply. AI use is explicit and often welcomed. Cath Ellis’s FIRE refinement specifies Lane 2 should be Formative, Instant-feedback, Repeatable, and build Evaluative judgement.

This move stops faculty from trying to AI-proof every assignment. Security investment is finite, and spreading it thinly across every task produces friction without protection. Concentrating it in specific Lane 1 moments frees the much larger Lane 2 surface for experimentation and AI-aware learning.

Where to look

  • University of Sydney Two-Lane operational guide — the public implementation document with worked examples.
  • Opposite of Cheating, Ep 28 — Danny Liu and Ep 36 — Cath Ellis.
  • Starting move: identify the one or two moments in your course where a credential-defensible claim about student learning is made. Everything else is Lane 2.

How they compose: TILT tells you whether an assignment is well-designed for learning. Two-Lane tells you where it belongs and the stakes. Held together, they yield a single design sentence for every assignment: “This is a Lane [1 or 2] assignment, scoring [Exemplary / Accomplished / Emerging] on TILT.”

The nine strategies

Each move, with why it works, how to do it, and where to look

1

Set an explicit AI-use policy for each assignment, not just the syllabus

Explicit expectations

Why this works

Blanket “AI is prohibited” or “AI is allowed” statements at the syllabus level don’t help students make good choices on specific tasks. Per-assignment guidance (“AI may be used for X, not for Y, and here’s why”) treats students as capable of ethical judgment. It removes the ambiguity that leads to quiet, unspoken decisions about AI use, and reduces the chance of a student following the rules for course A while doing work for course B. Faculty are converging on a lightweight vocabulary for tagging AI use per task: the AI Assessment Scale (AIAS), ranging from Level 1 (No AI) to Level 5 (Full AI), or a simpler three-color Stoplight version.

How to actually do it

  • Adopt a simple tagging system for each assignment: the AIAS (five levels) or the Stoplight approach (Red / Yellow / Green), which is simpler for entry-level adoption.
  • On the assignment description, include one sentence: “AI use on this task: [level]. Reasoning: [1 sentence].”
  • Ask students to disclose AI use in a brief note at the end of the submission, regardless of level.

Where to look

  • Mike Perkins’s AIAS custom GPT — walks you through tagging your own assignments.
  • Ronald Lethcoe’s Applied AI crosswalk — maps assignment types to AI integration levels using the stoplight framework.
  • Lance Eaton’s crowdsourced AI syllabus/policies compilation — real syllabus language from many institutions.

2

Open the term with short, unscripted student video or audio introductions

Presence & relationship

Why this works

A 60–90 second unscripted intro establishes each student’s voice, identity, and presence for the instructor, for peers, and for the AI-integrity design of the entire course. Later assignments can reference these introductions. Students who introduce themselves at the start invest differently in the course going forward, and it signals early that this is a course where the student is the primary voice.

How to actually do it

  • Use your LMS’s built-in video/audio recorder (Canvas Studio, Panopto, and Kaltura all support this).
  • Prompt: “Introduce yourself in 60 to 90 seconds. Tell us your name, one thing you hope to learn, and one thing about your context (job, family, hobby, place) that shapes why this course matters to you.” Unscripted means no slides, no reading.
  • Grade for completion, not polish.

Where to look

  • Flower Darby’s website — practical resources on establishing online-teacher presence in async environments.
  • Opposite of Cheating, Ep 13 — Flower Darby — the case for human-teacher presence as integrity infrastructure.

3

Build in at least one brief synchronous or semi-synchronous interaction per module

Presence & relationship

Why this works

A recurring finding in the academic-integrity research literature is that known students cheat less. A brief 1:1 or small-group interaction per module, even 10 minutes, establishes the human relationship that makes shortcuts feel wrong. When synchronous doesn’t scale, semi-synchronous alternatives (recorded video responses, threaded voice discussions, structured office-hour sign-ups) achieve much of the same effect.

How to actually do it

  • If your section is small enough: offer 10-minute 1:1 video check-ins at one or more points in the term.
  • If your section is larger: use peer groups of 3 or 4 with structured discussion prompts and, if available, rotating facilitators.
  • Semi-synchronous alternative: students record 2-minute video reflections on a weekly prompt; peers respond via video.

Where to look

  • Higher Ed AI Writing Facebook group (Laura Dumin) — an active practitioner community sharing async-course engagement patterns.
  • Opposite of Cheating, Ep 24 — Laura Dumin — strategies for async courses where relationships still work.

4

Embed brief (1–2 minute) low-stakes oral concept explanations within quizzes or activities

Observed cognition

Why this works

This is the “micro-oral,” a cheaper and more scalable cousin of full oral exams. Students record a 1-minute video explaining their reasoning on a specific concept or answer choice. The recording produces evidence of understanding that AI cannot fabricate at speed, and it focuses the assessment on the student’s reasoning rather than the correctness of the answer alone. Students report that explaining aloud clarifies their own thinking.

How to actually do it

  • Add a “record your explanation” prompt to 3 to 5 quiz questions per module. Use Canvas Studio or a video-response feature.
  • Score for evidence of reasoning: did the student explain why, not just repeat the answer? Two or three rubric criteria is enough.
  • Weight low (5–10% of the course grade) so students engage without high stakes.

Where to look

  • Will Styler’s public oral exam protocol — built for longer orals, but the rubric approach scales down to 1-minute micro-orals.
  • UCSD Oral Exam Project (HuiHui Qi) — public materials from 200+ student engineering courses.
  • Opposite of Cheating, Ep 1 — Will Styler and Ep 11 — HuiHui Qi.

5

Design assignments that require local, personal, or course-specific context

Task design that resists AI substitution

Why this works

Assignments that require external context (this student’s workplace, this student’s community, a specific real-world case the student is close to) are harder for AI to complete without the student’s active involvement. AI can produce a generic answer; it cannot produce the details of a workplace situation or a lived experience. Context becomes the friction that turns AI from a substitute into a supporting tool. Read this together with Strategy 6, which draws context from inside the course instead.

How to actually do it

  • Instead of “Analyze a company’s marketing strategy,” try: “Analyze the marketing of a company you have personally interacted with in the past month. Include specific details about your interaction as evidence.”
  • Ask students to draw on a real workplace or community setting: “Take a decision your organization made in the last 90 days and evaluate it against the framework we discussed.”
  • Give students choice of case: they identify the situation and defend its relevance.

Where to look

  • Opposite of Cheating, Ep 26 — Christopher Ostro — practical framing on personalized assignments.
  • Christopher Ostro’s open teaching-for-integrity materials — real assignment examples you can adapt.
  • Ep 59 — Joe Clare — the criminology-of-cheating case for why context reduces opportunity.

6

Personalize assignments by referencing students’ own prior work or contributions

Task design that resists AI substitution

Why this works

Where Strategy 5 pulls context from outside the course, this pulls it from inside. A prompt that references what this specific student wrote earlier in the term, or what a named classmate contributed to a Week 3 discussion, is one AI can help with only if the student actively brings that course-internal material into the conversation. It also builds a coherent arc: students see their own thinking developing over time, which strengthens intrinsic motivation.

How to actually do it

  • Design a mid-term or final that requires the student to build on, extend, or challenge their own Week 3 (or earlier) submission.
  • Reference class discussions: “In our Week 3 discussion, we distinguished X from Y. Apply that distinction to a case from your own experience.”
  • Structure peer review as a substantive response task: “Read [named classmate’s] Week 5 project. Identify the argument’s strongest and weakest point, and defend your evaluation with specifics.”
  • For discussion boards, require students to explicitly cite and respond to two named classmates’ posts.

Where to look

  • HuiHui Qi’s Engineering Pedagogy and Design Lab — practical patterns for large-enrollment courses.
  • Opposite of Cheating, Ep 11 — HuiHui Qi — the operational case that this works even at 200+ students.

7

Make process and response to feedback part of what students submit

Process & feedback

Why this works

AI can generate a polished final artifact in seconds. What it cannot easily fabricate is a coherent process: decisions made along the way, drafts revised, resources consulted, feedback engaged with. When you require visible process evidence, the assignment becomes something the student has to actually do, and you get formative evidence you can act on before the final grade. Research also shows that revising work in response to formative feedback deepens learning.

How to actually do it

  • Break one major assignment into two or three milestones (proposal → draft → final). Provide specific feedback at each stage.
  • Require intermediate artifacts: an outline before the draft, a draft before the final, a decision log alongside a project.
  • Require a response memo with the final: “Here’s how I addressed each piece of feedback I received.” One paragraph per major point.
  • Ask for a 200-word process reflection describing what changed between draft and final and why. Grade the response to feedback alongside the artifact.

Where to look

  • Emily Pitts Donahoe’s Unmaking the Grade — an accessible practitioner reference for iterative grading.
  • Sharing my course documents — her actual working course materials, and Opposite of Cheating, Ep 40.
  • Joseph Thibault’s Cursive Technology (writing-process capture) with Ep 9, and Carter Moulton’s Analog Inspiration deck.

8

Design at least one assignment for a real audience beyond the instructor

Task design that resists AI substitution

Why this works

When students write for a real audience (a specific community, a workplace, a client, a public forum), the definition of “good work” becomes more concrete and the stakes become more meaningful. Real-audience assignments also tend to require context and voice that AI cannot easily fake. As Torrey Trust frames it: “AI can do this. Why am I asking them to do this?” If the answer is “because a real person needs it,” the assignment justifies itself.

How to actually do it

  • Partner with a campus or community organization for a project-based assignment: students produce something the partner will actually use.
  • Ask students to produce work for a specific public forum (a blog post, a policy brief, a community presentation, a professional portfolio piece).
  • Give students choice of audience: they identify the recipient and defend the fit.

Where to look

  • Torrey Trust’s website — practical guidance on authentic-assessment design.
  • Opposite of Cheating, Ep 34 — Torrey Trust — the framing question and how she applies it.
  • Jason Gulya’s Higher AI — writing-course examples of real-audience design in AI-integrated courses.

9

Establish at least one secured “assurance” or “Lane 1” moment per course

Observed cognition

Why this works

This comes directly from the Two-Lane model. If a course grants a credential, it needs at least one moment where the institution can be confident the student actually did the work: not AI, not a friend, not a paid service. This is not about turning every assignment into a proctored exam. It is about designing one Lane 1 checkpoint per course (an oral defense, an in-person presentation, a supervised task, or a proctored capstone) that anchors everything else. At ASU, this provides assurance of learning in the accreditation sense while avoiding a vexing game of cat and mouse: a defensible answer to “how do you know?” without asking every task to bear that weight.

How to actually do it

  • Identify the one or two moments where a claim about student learning matters most. Redesign those as observed assessments: an 8-minute oral, a supervised in-person task, or a synchronous defense.
  • If your course is fully async and your institution supports it: use proctored remote orals or a virtual defense session at term end.
  • Communicate the assurance moment early and clearly: this is where the credential is staked, and here is how students can prepare.

Where to look

  • Will Styler’s oral exam protocol — the public template most widely adapted.
  • HuiHui Qi’s UCSD Oral Exam Project — proof this works at scale.
  • University of Sydney Two-Lane guide — the programmatic case for concentrating assurance in specific moments.

How they fit together

You do not need to adopt all nine. One approach is to pick one strategy from three different clusters: an explicit AI policy (Strategy 1), a presence move (Strategy 2 or 3), and an observed-cognition move (Strategy 4 or 9). Combining those three often produces the largest change with the least redesign effort. Task-design strategies (5, 6, 8) and process and feedback (7) then deepen the redesign where it matters most for your course.
