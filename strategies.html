Nine practical strategies for assessment design in the age of AI

Two foundational frameworks

The frameworks that anchor these strategies

The strategies fit into two composable frameworks from established faculty practice. Neither is required for adopting any single strategy, but holding both in mind sharpens where to invest redesign effort.

TILT

Transparency in Learning and Teaching

Developed by Dr. Mary-Ann Winkelmes, TILT is a small, high-leverage change to how assignments are written. Many students, especially those first in their family to attend college, struggle not because the work is too hard but because the unwritten rules of the assignment are unclear. TILT fixes that by making three things explicit on every assignment. It is deliberately lightweight: transparency, not more work.

  • Purpose — why am I doing this? The skills students practice, the knowledge they gain, and how it connects to real life and course outcomes.
  • Task — what exactly do I do? The steps to follow, what to do and what to avoid, and any productive-struggle steps left open on purpose.
  • Criteria — how is it judged? A checklist or rubric, what a successful piece looks like, and annotated examples of real work.

An AAC&U pilot (2014–15) across 7 minority-serving institutions, 1,180 students and 35 faculty, found that applying TILT to just two assignments produced statistically significant gains of medium-to-large magnitude in academic confidence, sense of belonging, and skill mastery, with the largest gains for first-generation, low-income, and underserved students (Winkelmes et al., Peer Review, AAC&U, 2016).

TILT clarity is the precondition for everything else, and it pairs directly with Two-Lane: once a task sits in the open lane, TILT is where you make the AI rules explicit. The Task section states exactly how students may use AI, and the Criteria reward their judgment over the raw AI output.

Where to look
  • Checklist for Designing Transparent Assignments (TILT Higher Ed, PDF) — a one-page checklist to hold your own assignments up against.
  • Measuring Transparency: A Learning-Focused Assignment Rubric — a 19-component instrument to audit your assignments (Palmer, Gravett, LaFleur 2018).
  • Starting move: start small. Restructure your two highest-stakes assignments as Purpose / Task / Criteria, often just reorganizing what already exists. The research shows the effect from TILTing as few as two.
Two-Lane

Assessment must do two jobs at once

From the University of Sydney (Danny Liu and Adam Bridgeman, 2024–2025). The premise: assessment now has to do two jobs at once. It must assure that students actually achieved the outcomes, and it must help students learn to work productively with AI. One assessment type cannot do both jobs well, so the model sorts every assessment into one of two lanes.

Our context, online and asynchronous. Lane 1 is defined by controlled conditions, not by a physical room, so the model fits async online directly. Online, we achieve “controlled” through course and platform design. The specific methods are an area our design team continues to refine.
Lane 1 — Controlled (Assessment OF Learning). Conditions controlled enough to assure the work reflects this student’s own capability. It is about conditions and purpose, not location. Controlled does not mean AI-banned: AI may be restricted, or deliberately permitted in specified ways. Options include identity-verified time-boxed tasks and authenticated performance work. Purpose: a defensible signal of genuine achievement.
Lane 2 — Open (Assessment FOR / AS Learning). Uncontrolled conditions where AI use is permitted and even encouraged. The focus shifts from policing AI to building AI literacy; students engage with AI output rather than just submitting it. Guide AI use as a menu, not a traffic light, and make thinking visible through process artifacts (drafts, chat logs, reflections). Purpose: build AI-capable graduates.

The key move: assign lanes at the assessment level. Lane assignment is a course-level, assessment-by-assessment decision guided by each task’s purpose (program-level coordination is optional). Put controlled assessment only where an outcome genuinely needs to be assured, and let most of the course live in the open lane. A single task can combine both, for example an open project capped by a short controlled check.

Common misreads to avoid
  • Lane 1 is not “in person” and not “no AI.” It means controlled conditions to assure authorship; AI may be restricted or purposefully permitted within them.
  • Lane 2 is not “unsecured” or a free-for-all. Open tasks are deliberately designed for critical, responsible AI use.
  • Don’t over-control. Controlling every task raises cost and anxiety and crowds out authentic learning. Aim for proportion, not maximum surveillance.
Where to look
  • University of Sydney Two-Lane operational guide (Liu & Bridgeman) — the public implementation document, with worked examples and FAQ.
  • Opposite of Cheating, Ep 28 — Danny Liu — the conversation that grounds the model.
  • Starting move: for each assessment, decide whether the outcome needs controlled conditions (Lane 1) or is best developed in the open lane (Lane 2). Label it clearly for students.

How they compose

Two-Lane decides which lane a task lives in, controlled (Lane 1) or open (Lane 2). TILT makes the rules of that lane explicit to students, especially the open lane, where “how may I use AI here, and how will it be graded?” must be stated plainly. Together they yield one design summary for every assessment: “This is a Lane [1 or 2] assignment, scored on Purpose / Task / Criteria.”

Explicit expectations

Strategy 1
1

Set an explicit AI-use policy for each assignment, not just the syllabus

Why it works

Blanket “AI is prohibited” or “AI is allowed” statements at the syllabus level don’t help students make good choices on specific tasks. Per-assignment guidance treats students as capable of ethical judgment, removes the ambiguity that leads to quiet decisions about AI use, and keeps the rules for one course from bleeding into another. Faculty are converging on a shared vocabulary: the AI Assessment Scale (AIAS), Level 1 (No AI) through Level 5 (Full AI), or a simpler Stoplight (Red / Yellow / Green).

How to do it
  • Adopt one tagging system: the five-level AIAS, or the simpler Red / Yellow / Green Stoplight for entry-level adoption.
  • On each assignment, add one sentence: “AI use on this task: [level]. Reasoning: [1 sentence].”
  • Ask students to disclose AI use in a brief note at the end of the submission, regardless of level.
Where to look
  • Ronald Lethcoe’s Applied AI in the College Classroom (Canvas course) — module-by-module examples of AI-integrated assignment design, including a syllabus-enhancement session.
  • Crowdsourced AI syllabus and course policies (Google Sheet) — real syllabus language from many institutions, maintained by Lance Eaton and community.

Presence & relationship

Strategies 2 & 3
2

Open the term with short, unscripted student video or audio introductions

Why it works

A 60–90 second unscripted intro establishes each student’s voice, identity, and presence for the instructor, for peers, and for the integrity design of the whole course. Later assignments can reference these introductions, and students who introduce themselves invest differently going forward. It signals early that this is a course where the student is the primary voice.

How to do it
  • Use your LMS’s built-in recorder (Canvas Studio, Panopto, Kaltura all support this).
  • Prompt: “Introduce yourself in 60 to 90 seconds: your name, one thing you hope to learn, and one thing about your context that shapes why this course matters.” No slides, no reading.
  • Grade for completion, not polish.
Where to look
  • Opposite of Cheating, Ep 13 — Flower Darby — the case for human-teacher presence as integrity infrastructure.
3

Build in at least one brief synchronous or semi-synchronous interaction per module

Why it works

A recurring finding in the integrity literature is that known students cheat less. A brief 1:1 or small-group interaction per module, even 10 minutes, builds the human relationship that makes shortcuts feel wrong. When synchronous doesn’t scale, semi-synchronous alternatives (recorded video responses, threaded voice discussions, structured office-hour sign-ups) achieve much of the same effect.

How to do it
  • Small sections: offer 10-minute 1:1 video check-ins at one or more points in the term.
  • Larger sections: use peer groups of 3 or 4 with structured prompts and, if available, rotating facilitators.
  • Semi-synchronous: students record 2-minute reflections on a weekly prompt; peers respond via video.
Where to look
  • Higher Ed AI Writing Facebook group (Laura Dumin) — an active practitioner community sharing async-engagement patterns.
  • Opposite of Cheating, Ep 24 — Laura Dumin — strategies for async courses where relationships still work.

Observed cognition

Strategy 4 (Lane 1 or Lane 2, depending on how it’s deployed)
4

Embed brief (1–2 minute) low-stakes oral concept explanations within quizzes or activities

Why it works

This is the “micro-oral,” a cheaper and more scalable cousin of the full oral exam. A student records a 1-minute video explaining their reasoning on a concept or answer choice. It produces evidence of understanding AI cannot fabricate at speed, and it focuses the assessment on reasoning rather than the answer alone. Students report that explaining aloud clarifies their own thinking.

How to do it
  • Add a “record your explanation” prompt to 3 to 5 quiz questions per module. Use Canvas Studio or a video-response feature.
  • Score for evidence of reasoning: did the student explain why, not just repeat the answer? Two or three criteria is enough.
  • Weight low (5–10% of the grade) so students engage without high stakes.
Where to look
  • Will Styler’s public oral exam protocol — built for longer orals, but the rubric approach scales to 1-minute micro-orals.
  • UCSD Oral Exam Project (HuiHui Qi) — public materials from 200+ student engineering courses.
  • Opposite of Cheating, Ep 1 — Will Styler and Ep 11 — HuiHui Qi.

Task design that resists AI substitution

Strategies 5, 6 & 8
5

Design assignments that require local, personal, or course-specific context

Why it works

Assignments that require external context (this student’s workplace, community, or a real case they are close to) are harder for AI to complete without the student’s active involvement. AI can produce a generic answer; it cannot produce the details of a specific workplace situation or lived experience. Context becomes the friction that turns AI from a substitute into a supporting tool. Read alongside Strategy 6, which draws context from inside the course.

How to do it
  • Instead of “Analyze a company’s marketing strategy,” try: “Analyze the marketing of a company you have personally interacted with in the past month. Include specific details as evidence.”
  • Draw on a real setting: “Take a decision your organization made in the last 90 days and evaluate it against our framework.”
  • Give students choice of case: they identify the situation and defend its relevance.
Where to look
  • Opposite of Cheating, Ep 26 — Christopher Ostro — practical framing on personalized assignments.
  • Christopher Ostro’s open teaching-for-integrity materials — real assignment examples you can adapt.
  • Ep 59 — Joe Clare — the criminology-of-cheating case for why context reduces opportunity.
6

Personalize assignments by referencing students’ own prior work or contributions

Why it works

Where Strategy 5 pulls context from outside the course, this pulls it from inside. A prompt that references what this student wrote earlier, or what a named classmate contributed in Week 3, is one AI can help with only if the student actively brings that course-internal material in. It also builds a coherent arc: students see their own thinking develop over time, which strengthens intrinsic motivation.

How to do it
  • Design a mid-term or final that requires building on, extending, or challenging the student’s own earlier submission.
  • Reference class discussions: “In Week 3 we distinguished X from Y. Apply that to a case from your own experience.”
  • Make peer review substantive: “Identify the strongest and weakest point in [named classmate’s] project, and defend your evaluation with specifics.”
  • On discussion boards, require students to cite and respond to two named classmates’ posts.
Where to look
  • HuiHui Qi’s Engineering Pedagogy and Design Lab — practical patterns for large-enrollment courses.
  • Opposite of Cheating, Ep 11 — HuiHui Qi — the operational case that this works even at 200+ students.
8

Design at least one assignment for a real audience beyond the instructor

Why it works

When students write for a real audience (a community, a workplace, a client, a public forum), the definition of “good work” becomes concrete and the stakes become meaningful. Real-audience assignments also tend to require context and voice AI cannot easily fake. As Torrey Trust frames it: “AI can do this. Why am I asking them to do this?” If the answer is “because a real person needs it,” the assignment justifies itself.

How to do it
  • Partner with a campus or community organization: students produce something the partner will actually use.
  • Aim work at a specific public forum: a blog post, a policy brief, a community presentation, a portfolio piece.
  • Give students choice of audience: they identify the recipient and defend the fit.
Where to look
  • Torrey Trust’s Design Projects page — examples of authentic, project-based assessment designs from her courses.
  • Opposite of Cheating, Ep 34 — Torrey Trust — the framing question and how she applies it.
  • Jason Gulya, “AI and Alternative Assessment, Redux” — what real-audience assessment looks like in AI-integrated writing courses.

Process & feedback as part of the deliverable

Strategy 7
7

Make process and response to feedback part of what students submit

Why it works

AI can generate a polished final artifact in seconds. What it cannot easily fabricate is a coherent process: decisions made, drafts revised, resources consulted, feedback engaged with. Requiring visible process evidence makes the assignment something the student has to actually do, and gives you formative evidence to act on before the final grade. Revising in response to feedback also deepens learning.

How to do it
  • Break one major assignment into milestones (proposal → draft → final), with specific feedback at each stage.
  • Require intermediate artifacts: an outline before the draft, a decision log alongside a project.
  • Require a response memo: “Here’s how I addressed each piece of feedback,” one paragraph per point.
  • Ask for a 200-word process reflection on what changed between draft and final, and grade it alongside the artifact.
Where to look
  • Emily Pitts Donahoe’s Unmaking the Grade — an accessible reference for iterative and collaborative grading.
  • Sharing my course documents — her working course materials, and Opposite of Cheating, Ep 40.
  • Ep 9 — Joseph Thibault on writing process as primary evidence, and Carter Moulton’s Analog Inspiration deck.

Lane 1 controlled assurance

Strategy 9
9

Establish at least one “controlled” (Lane 1) assessment where the student’s own capability is assured

Why it works

This comes directly from the Two-Lane model. If a course grants a credential, at least one assessment needs to happen under conditions controlled enough to assure the work reflects this student’s own capability. Controlled does not mean AI-banned, and it does not mean in-person. It means conditions designed to provide a defensible signal of genuine achievement. Once that assurance moment exists, the rest of the course can live comfortably in the open lane where real learning and AI fluency develop.

Why it matters at ASU. This provides assurance of learning in the accreditation sense while avoiding a vexing game of cat and mouse. It gives the institution a defensible answer to “how do you know?” without asking every task to bear that weight. Because “controlled” is about conditions and not location, the model fits async online directly, and the specific methods are an area our design team is continuing to refine.

How to do it
  • For each assessment, decide whether the outcome needs controlled conditions (Lane 1) or is best developed in the open lane (Lane 2). Aim for at least one Lane 1 assessment; more is fine when the outcomes warrant it.
  • Choose the control method deliberately: identity-verified time-boxed tasks, authenticated performance work (oral defense, live demonstration, synchronous check-in), or another fit-for-purpose approach with your instructional-design partner.
  • Decide what AI role, if any, is appropriate within the controlled conditions. Controlled and “AI-permitted-in-specified-ways” can coexist.
  • Communicate the Lane 1 assessment early: this is where the credential is staked, and here is how students can prepare.
Where to look
  • Will Styler’s oral exam protocol — the public template most widely adapted for authenticated performance work.
  • HuiHui Qi’s UCSD Oral Exam Project — proof this works at scale.
  • University of Sydney Two-Lane operational guide (Liu & Bridgeman) — the framework and worked examples.

How they fit together

Pick one move from a few clusters

You do not need all nine. One approach is to take one strategy from three different clusters:

  • Explicit expectations: Strategy 1.
  • Presence and relationship: Strategies 2 and 3.
  • Observed cognition (Lane 1 or Lane 2, depending on how it’s deployed): Strategy 4.
  • Task design that resists AI substitution (Lane 2): Strategies 5, 6, and 8.
  • Process and feedback as part of the deliverable (Lane 2): Strategy 7.
  • Lane 1 controlled assurance: Strategy 9.

Combining one move from each of the first three clusters (an explicit AI policy, a presence move, and an observed-cognition move) often produces the largest change with the least redesign effort.
